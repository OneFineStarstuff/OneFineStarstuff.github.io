# **Monograph Submission: The Semantic Quotient Functor**\n\n## **3.2 The Semantic Quotient Functor `Q`**\n\nIn Section 3.1, we established that any morphism `f: (X, I) → (Y, J)` in `\\mathbf{Interp}` gives rise to a canonical map `f_S: X/{\\simI} → Y/{\\simJ}` between the corresponding semantic quotients. This canonical assignment is the foundation for a functor that maps the entire category of interpreted systems to the category of sets, effectively automating the process of semantic reduction.\n\n**Definition 3.2.1 (The Semantic Quotient Functor).** The **Semantic Quotient Functor**, denoted `Q: \\mathbf{Interp} → \\mathbf{Set}`, is defined by its assignments on objects and morphisms as follows:\n\n-   **Object Assignment:** For any object `(X, I)` in `\\mathbf{Interp}`, `Q` maps it to its semantic quotient:\n    `Q((X, I)) = X/{\\simI}`.\n\n-   **Morphism Assignment:** For any morphism `f: (X, I) → (Y, J)` in `\\mathbf{Interp}`, `Q` maps it to the unique arrow `f_S` in `\\mathbf{Set}` whose existence is guaranteed by Corollary 3.1.4:\n    `Q(f) = f_S: X/{\\simI} → Y/{\\simJ}`.\n\n**Proposition 3.2.2 (Well-Definedness and Functoriality of `Q`).** The mapping `Q` is a well-defined functor.\n\n*Proof.* The well-definedness of the morphism assignment is guaranteed by the universal property invoked in the proof of Corollary 3.1.4, which ensures that `f_S` is unique for every `f`. To prove functoriality, we must show that `Q` preserves identity morphisms and composition.\n\n1.  **Preservation of Identities:** Let `id_X: (X, I) → (X, I)` be an identity morphism in `\\mathbf{Interp}`. We must show that `Q(id_X)` is the identity morphism `id_{X/{\simI}}` in `\\mathbf{Set}`. By definition, `Q(id_X)` is the unique map that makes `\qI ∘ id_X = Q(id_X) ∘ \qI` commute. Since `id_{X/{\simI}}` trivially satisfies this condition (`\qI = id_{X/{\simI}} ∘ \qI`), by uniqueness, `Q(id_X) = id_{X/{\simI}}`.\n\n2.  **Preservation of Composition:** Let `f: (X, I) → (Y, J)` and `g: (Y, J) → (Z, K)` be two composable morphisms in `\\mathbf{Interp}`. We must show `Q(g ∘ f) = Q(g) ∘ Q(f)`. By definition, these are the maps `(g ∘ f)_S`, `g_S`, and `f_S`. The defining property of `(g ∘ f)_S` is the unique commutativity of the outer rectangle in the diagram below. The properties of `f_S` and `g_S` define the commutativity of the left and right squares, respectively. \n\n    ```mermaid\n    graph TD\n        X -- f --> Y -- g --> Z;\n        X -- \qI --> X/{\simI};\n        Y -- \qJ --> Y/{\simJ};\n        Z -- \qK --> Z/{\simK};\n        X/{\simI} -- f_S --> Y/{\simJ} -- g_S --> Z/{\simK};\n        X -- g ∘ f --> Z;\n        X/{\simI} -.-> |(g ∘ f)_S| Z/{\simK};\n    ```\n\n    The path `(\qK ∘ g) ∘ f` is equal to `(g_S ∘ \qJ) ∘ f = g_S ∘ (\qJ ∘ f) = g_S ∘ (f_S ∘ \qI) = (g_S ∘ f_S) ∘ \qI`. Thus, the map `g_S ∘ f_S` makes the outer diagram commute. By the uniqueness of the induced map for `g ∘ f`, it must be that `(g ∘ f)_S = g_S ∘ f_S`. Therefore, `Q(g ∘ f) = Q(g) ∘ Q(f)`. □\n\n### **3.3 The Role of `Q` in Semantic Representation**\n\n**Interpretation 3.3.1 (Canonical Representation).** The functor `Q` provides the canonical representation of interpreted systems up to semantic equivalence. It maps each system to its essential semantic content (the quotient set) and transforms every structure-preserving map between systems into the corresponding map between their semantic contents. Two systems `(X, I)` and `(Y, J)` are **semantically equivalent** if `Q((X, I))` is isomorphic to `Q((Y, J))` in `\\mathbf{Set}`, meaning their underlying sets of meanings have the same cardinality.\n\n**Proposition 3.3.2 (Factorization Through Quotients).** Every morphism `f: (X, I) → (Y, J)` in `\\mathbf{Interp}` gives rise to a commutative square:\n\n`\qJ ∘ f = Q(f) ∘ \qI`\n\nThis is not merely a property but the very *definition* of `Q(f)`. This shows that the action of any semantic morphism can be viewed as first reducing the source space `X` to its canonical semantic form `X/{\simI}` via `\qI`, and then applying the purely semantic map `Q(f)`.\n\n**Proposition 3.3.3 (Preservation and Reflection of Properties).**\n\n-   **Preservation:** The functor `Q` **preserves** isomorphisms. If `f` is an isomorphism in `\\mathbf{Interp}`, then `Q(f)` is an isomorphism in `\\mathbf{Set}` (a bijection).\n-   **Reflection:** `Q` does **not** reflect isomorphisms. A morphism `f` can induce a bijection `Q(f)` on the semantic quotients even if `f` itself is not an isomorphism (e.g., if `f` is not injective but maps distinct states with the same meaning to distinct states in `Y` that also have the same meaning).\n\nThis one-way preservation is characteristic of functors that abstract or \"forget\" information—in this case, syntactic detail.\n
